Teaching a Unitree R1 to walk to an obstacle in the KUKA cell

A single legged humanoid, trained from scratch in NVIDIA Isaac Sim, that walks upright across the robotic cell and stops a few centimetres short of the obstacle — and the reward-hacking bug we had to defeat to get there.

Robot Unitree R1 (bipedal) Engine Isaac Sim 5.1 / Isaac Lab Algorithm PPO · rsl_rl Hardware RTX 5070 Ti

The goal

What we set out to build

One Unitree R1 humanoid placed inside the KUKA robotic cell, alongside the obstacles from before — the KUKA robot and a ball. The R1 should walk toward the ball and stop a few centimetres short of it, without colliding and without falling over.

The catch that made this hard: the R1 is a legged bipedal robot, not the wheeled robot used in the earlier navigation work. A legged robot has no wheels to coast on — it falls unless a control policy actively balances it every millisecond. So before it can navigate anywhere, it has to learn the single hardest skill in the project: walking.

Signal

The discovery — it learned to cheat

Attempt 2 looked like a success on paper. To be sure, we ran a headless evaluation that measures behaviour, not reward — commanding 64 robots forward and logging what fraction stay upright. The result was damning.

Torso held at 0.72 m (full standing height) for the entire run, walking forward at 0.93 m/s. Upright the whole time.

Why it happened. The reward paid out for forward motion regardless of posture, the "upright" reward was switched off, and the fall-detector only fired if the torso hit the ground — which a low crawler's torso never does. So crawling was a stable, rewarded strategy, and more training simply perfected the crawl.

03

The fix — making cheating impossible

We rewrote the reward and termination rules so that falling can never pay off, then retrained from scratch:

  • End the episode the instant it falls — if the torso drops below 0.5 m or tips past ~46°, the run is over. A crawler now dies immediately and earns nothing.
  • Reward staying upright — turned the orientation penalty back on.
  • Reward staying tall — added a penalty for straying from 0.72 m standing height.

The early-training signal confirmed the fix immediately: episodes started short (robots fall and terminate at ~29 steps) and lengthened as the policy learned to balance — reaching the full 1,000-step episode by the end. The only way to score was now to actually walk.

05

How the stopping works

The behaviour is split into two layers. This matters because it's the foundation everything next builds on.

High level · a rule
Where to go
Measures the distance to the ball and outputs a velocity command — "walk forward," ramping to "stop" as it nears the ball.
→
Low level · the RL policy
How to move
Turns that velocity command into joint torques that walk — or, on a zero command, stand balanced. This is the part we trained.

The policy never "sees" the ball. It only ever receives a velocity command. When the high-level rule zeroes that command near the obstacle, the policy holds the robot upright and still — which is why it stops and stays standing.

Lessons

What this taught us

  • Never trust reward alone. A rising reward can mean a better cheat. Always measure the behaviour you actually want — here, the fraction of time spent upright.
  • Reward hacking is real and subtle. The crawl wasn't a bug in the code; it was the honest optimum of a reward that forgot to require standing.
  • Termination design shapes behaviour as much as rewards. Ending the episode on a fall is what forced walking to emerge.
  • Iteration count was a red herring. The first instinct — "train longer" — would never have fixed a policy optimizing the wrong thing.

Next

Where this goes

  • Learned obstacle avoidance. Right now the walking is RL but the decision to stop is a hand-written rule. Next: train a high-level navigation policy that perceives the ball and learns to slow and stop on its own — riding on top of this walker.
  • The HuggingFace / LeRobot pipeline. Record the R1's walk as a LeRobot-format dataset — the bridge toward fine-tuning NVIDIA's GR00T foundation model on this robot.
